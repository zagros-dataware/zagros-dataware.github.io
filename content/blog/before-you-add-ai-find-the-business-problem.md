AI has become an increasingly common part of conversations about business growth, efficiency and digital transformation.

But there is a question that should come before the technology:

**What business problem are we actually trying to solve?**

This distinction matters because the same business symptom can have very different causes—and therefore require very different interventions.

A slow process may need redesign.

A repetitive task may need automation.

Disconnected systems may need integration.

Poor decisions may require better data.

And in some cases, AI may genuinely be the appropriate intervention.

The technology should follow the evidence.

## **Start with the business problem**

Instead of starting with:

> “Where can we use AI?”

start with:

> “What is happening in the business that we want to improve?”

Look for problems such as:

* repetitive manual work  
* slow response times  
* difficult information retrieval  
* inconsistent decisions  
* large amounts of unstructured information  
* customer questions that require repeated responses  
* difficulty identifying patterns across data  
* processes that depend heavily on individual employees

But identifying a symptom isn't enough.

You need to understand the problem behind it.

## **Find the evidence**

Ask:

**How often does the problem occur?**

A task that happens once a month may not justify the same intervention as one happening hundreds of times every day.

**Who experiences the problem?**

Employees? Customers? Managers? Suppliers?

**What does it cost?**

The cost may be financial, but it could also appear as delays, errors, lost opportunities or poor customer experience.

**What information exists?**

Documents, transactions, conversations, historical records, product information and operational data may all be relevant.

Evidence turns a general technology idea into a specific business question.

## **Find the opportunity**

Once the problem is understood, ask what improvement is possible.

Could the business:

* reduce processing time?  
* reduce errors?  
* lower operational cost?  
* improve access to information?  
* respond to customers faster?  
* support employees with better information?  
* identify patterns that were previously difficult to see?  
* make a decision more consistently?

These are business outcomes.

AI is only one possible way to achieve them.

## **Compare the interventions**

Consider the simplest appropriate intervention first.

### **Process improvement**

If the process itself is unnecessarily complicated, adding technology may simply make a bad process faster.

### **Automation**

If the work follows predictable rules, conventional automation may be sufficient.

### **Integration**

If employees are repeatedly moving information between systems, connecting those systems may solve the problem.

### **Better data**

If decisions are difficult because information is incomplete, inconsistent or inaccessible, improving the data foundation may come first.

### **AI**

AI becomes particularly interesting when the problem involves activities such as understanding, generating, classifying, extracting, summarizing, searching or reasoning over information where traditional approaches are less effective or less flexible.

The important question is not whether AI *can* perform a task.

It is whether using AI creates a meaningful business improvement.

## **Build an AI business case**

When AI appears to be appropriate, make the case concrete.

Ask:

**What will change?**

Define the task, decision or experience that AI will affect.

**What will it cost?**

Consider development, integration, infrastructure, licensing, maintenance, training and operational oversight.

**What could improve?**

Define the expected business outcome.

**What risks need to be controlled?**

Accuracy, privacy, security, reliability, human oversight and operational failure may all matter depending on the use case.

**How will we measure success?**

Choose measurable indicators before implementation.

For example:

* processing time  
* cost per transaction  
* response time  
* error rate  
* employee time saved  
* customer satisfaction  
* conversion rate  
* resolution rate

  ## **Don't start with AI**

AI can be a powerful intervention.

But the starting point should remain the business problem.

A useful sequence is:

**Business Problem**  
**→ Evidence**  
**→ Opportunity**  
**→ Right Intervention**  
**→ AI Business Case**  
**→ Outcome**

This approach doesn't reject AI.

It puts AI in the right place: as a potential intervention that needs a business case.

The goal is not to add more technology.

The goal is to create a measurable improvement.

**Evidence Over Guesswork.**

If you're considering an AI initiative, start with the problem—not the model.
