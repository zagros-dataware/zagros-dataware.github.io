> **Representative scenario.** This walkthrough illustrates a common engagement pattern rather than a specific named client — the shape and figures are typical of this kind of work, not a disclosed case study.

## Starting point

A neighborhood restaurant takes nearly all of its orders by phone. During the dinner rush, the same one or two staff members who are cooking or running the front counter also have to stop to answer the phone, write down an order, and repeat it back for accuracy — the exact moment they can least afford the interruption. Some calls go unanswered during the busiest 90 minutes of the night, which means lost orders nobody ever sees.

## Mapping the real process, not just the menu

Before any design work starts, the actual order-to-plate process gets mapped: how an order comes in, how it reaches the kitchen, how substitutions and out-of-stock items get communicated back to the customer, how payment happens, how pickup or delivery timing is estimated. This surfaces the real constraint almost every time: the bottleneck isn't a lack of a website, it's that phone orders compete directly with in-person service for the same staff attention during the exact hours that matter most.

## Designing around kitchen capacity, not just checkout

A generic online-ordering template optimizes for the checkout flow and stops there. The more important design decisions are upstream of checkout:

- **Menu items marked unavailable in real time**, so a customer can't order something the kitchen already ran out of — the single most common source of a bad first online-ordering experience.
- **Order timing based on actual kitchen load**, not a fixed "30 minutes" estimate that's wrong during a rush and overcautious during a lull.
- **A clear, loud in-kitchen notification** for new online orders, so they land with the same urgency as an order called in or placed at the counter — an online order that sits unnoticed for ten minutes is worse than no online ordering at all.

## Fulfillment ownership, again

As with any local-business online-sales rollout, the technical build is the easier half. The harder, more consequential part is making sure a specific person owns "new online order arrived" as a task with a clear next step during service — the same operational-ownership question that determines whether a retailer's checkout button actually works on day one.

## What a result of this shape typically looks like

In engagements with this profile, online ordering typically absorbs a meaningful share of what would otherwise be missed phone calls during peak hours, and — often underappreciated going in — noticeably reduces order errors, since the customer is typing their own order and customizations instead of having them relayed over a phone line during a loud, busy service.

## Why this generalizes

Restaurants don't need a large ordering platform with more capability than they'll use. They need an ordering flow that respects the same real-time constraints — stock, kitchen load, staff attention — that already govern how the restaurant runs, built so that "online" and "in-person" don't quietly compete with each other during the hours business matters most.
