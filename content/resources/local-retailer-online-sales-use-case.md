> **Representative scenario.** This walkthrough illustrates a common engagement pattern rather than a specific named client — the shape and figures are typical of this kind of work, not a disclosed case study.

## Starting point

A local retailer has built a loyal walk-in customer base over several years but has no online presence beyond a social media page. Customers regularly ask, by message, whether an item is in stock, and staff answer each question manually. There's no way to browse, reserve, or buy anything outside store hours, and every question that arrives outside those hours goes unanswered until the next shift.

## Mapping the existing sale

Before any design work starts, the actual in-store sales process gets mapped the same way an enterprise workflow would: how does a customer currently find out what's available, how do they decide, how do they pay, how does the business fulfill the order. This typically surfaces that the real bottleneck isn't a lack of a website — it's that stock information lives only in the owner's head or a back-office spreadsheet nobody else can see.

## Designing the online flow around the real process

Rather than a generic catalog-and-checkout template, the site is built around the specific sequence this business's customers already go through: browse by category, see real availability, reserve or pay online, then pick up in store or receive delivery. Trust signals — real photos of the shop and product, customer reviews, clear pickup instructions — are placed before the buy button, not after it, since for a local business, trust in the physical location is usually the deciding factor over trust in the website itself.

## Fulfillment, not just checkout

A checkout button is only useful if the business can actually fulfill what it promises. Part of the engagement is typically making sure someone on staff owns "new online order" as a task with a clear next step, rather than a checkout flow being turned on without an operational owner behind it — the same failure mode a poorly rolled-out enterprise workflow hits, at a much smaller scale.

## What a result of this shape typically looks like

In engagements with this profile, the site typically launches within a few weeks — not months — because the scope is deliberately narrow: browse, check real availability, buy or reserve, pick up or deliver. Businesses in this situation commonly see a meaningful share of first online orders coming from existing walk-in customers simply switching to a more convenient channel, alongside new customers who would never have called to ask about stock but will browse a page.

## Why this generalizes

Local businesses rarely need a large custom e-commerce build to start selling online — they need the three or four steps a real customer already goes through, built cleanly and connected to a fulfillment process that actually works on day one.
